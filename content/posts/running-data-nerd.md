I got into running the same way I get into everything — by over-researching it. I didn't just lace up sneakers and go jogging. No, I spent weeks reading about heart rate zones, VO2max estimation algorithms, and the optimal pacing strategy for aerobic development. Then I bought a sports watch. Then a second one to compare data. Then I hit the pavement.

The first few months were glorious chaos. Strava became my social network, my diary, and my metrics dashboard all at once. I was obsessed with pace per kilometer across different heart rate zones. I calculated my lactate threshold pace based on maximum heart rate minus age (spoiler: that formula is garbage). I watched my estimated VO2max number like it was my stock portfolio, and yes, I did create a spreadsheet to track the trend.

I built a few scripts to pull Strava data via their API. Nothing fancy — just some bash and a bit of Python to see patterns. How many kilometers did I run in zone 2 last month? What's my average pace improvement over the past 12 weeks? On which days do I run fastest? I automated email digests. I created weekly summaries. I was treating my own body like a SaaS metrics dashboard.

Here's the thing though: all that data told me absolutely nothing that running itself wasn't already teaching me. The numbers confirmed what I felt. Easy runs were easy. Hard runs were hard. Some days I had energy, other days I didn't. The watch and the spreadsheets didn't change any of that.

But something interesting happened around month four. I stopped caring so much about the pace numbers and started paying attention to how running made me feel. Not my heart rate — my actual experience. Was I enjoying it? Could I hold a conversation? Did I sleep better that night? These questions mattered more than whether I hit my planned zone distribution. The data hadn't changed my running; running had changed what I thought the data meant.

I still track everything. I check Strava after every run. My watch is still synced to my phone, and yes, the data scripts still run. But I've deprioritized optimization. Running is not a product to iterate on. It's just... running. Sometimes it's fast, sometimes it's slow, sometimes I don't do it because I don't feel like it, and that's completely fine.

The geek in me hasn't disappeared — it's just found equilibrium with the human part. I can appreciate the elegance of a well-structured dataset while also understanding that the best run is the one you actually do, regardless of how it looks in a spreadsheet. The numbers are fun, they're motivating, and they help me see patterns over months. But they're not the point anymore.

Now if you'll excuse me, I need to update my weekly running summary script. It's been a few minutes and I'm pretty sure my zone distribution is off by a decimal point.
